warming

Start with the warming signal

A single hot year tells only part of the story. Begin with the national record, then compare how average daytime highs differ between earlier and recent periods across the capitals.

A century of changing temperatures

Annual mean temperature differences from the 1961–1990 average, 1910–2025. Red bars mean warmer years; blue bars mean cooler years.

Loading annual temperatures...

Recent warming is sustained, not just one exceptional year. The 2016–2025 average anomaly is +1.05°C relative to the 1961–1990 baseline. Annual ups and downs sit within that warmer recent period.

Why this chart?

A shared zero baseline lets you compare warmer and cooler years. Diverging bars reveal both exceptional years and sustained runs of positive anomalies. This is a temperature difference from 1961–1990, not an absolute temperature.

How do the capitals compare?

Compare average daytime highs in 1997–2010 and 2011–2024. Blue marks the earlier period; orange marks the recent one. Choose a season to see whether the differences change through the year.

Loading city temperatures...

Warming varies by place and season. Recent daytime highs are higher in 28 of the 32 city–season comparisons. Perth summer has the largest increase (+0.86°C); Canberra autumn has the largest decrease (−0.60°C). These compare two periods rather than measuring a continuous trend.

How to read this chart

Both periods contain 14 complete seasons at each selected station. Monthly means are weighted by calendar days, then seasonal means averaged across years. Summer includes December of the preceding year. These are average daytime highs, not record maximum temperatures.

Why this chart? Dumbbells make the direction and size of the difference visible through position and connecting-line length. Change the season to compare like with like; station-level results are not city-wide averages.

rainfall

One continent, different rainfall stories

Australia’s rainfall varies with place and season. First compare the broad patterns, then look more closely at unusually wet or dry months and the spread of annual totals.

Where does the rain fall?

Mean annual rainfall in millimetres, averaged over each decade. Change the decade to compare patterns on a fixed colour scale. Darker or more intense colours follow the rainfall legend.

Loading rainfall map...

Look for persistent regional differences. Compare the same location across decades before interpreting a colour change. A fixed scale makes these comparisons meaningful, but a decade average conceals unusually wet and dry individual years.

How to read this chart

The original grids are averaged into 1-degree display cells. All decades use the same colour scale. Coastal outlines are approximate.

Why this chart? The gridded map preserves geographic variation without assigning an entire state a single value. Colour shows rainfall amount; a large coloured region does not imply a large share of the population.

When does the rain arrive?

These zones distinguish summer-dominant, winter-dominant, uniform and arid rainfall patterns. Compare 30-year reference periods to explore how their mapped distribution differs.

Loading seasonal rainfall zones...

Timing matters alongside the annual total. Similar annual rainfall can arrive in different seasons. These categories describe the seasonal pattern; they do not measure the amount of change between periods. Adjacent reference periods share 20 years.

How to read this chart

Colours combine seasonal rainfall patterns with median annual rainfall bands. Hover for the zone and rainfall band. The classification compares November–April with May–October rainfall; it does not show individual seasonal totals. Adjacent reference periods overlap by 20 years.

Why this chart? A categorical area map helps locate seasonal regimes. Colours distinguish types rather than rank them from better to worse; changing colours is not by itself evidence of a statistically significant shift.

Which months were unusual?

Compare each month with the station’s 1991–2020 average for that calendar month. Brown means drier, teal means wetter and near-white means close to average. Grey means unavailable.

Loading monthly rainfall...

A wet month and a wet climate are different ideas. Look for runs of wet or dry months rather than drawing a conclusion from one intense cell. Comparisons are against each station’s own calendar-month average, so they show departures from usual conditions.

How to read this chart

Grey cells indicate missing observations or insufficient reference data. Reference averages require at least 20 quality-controlled years; Brisbane January/November and Hobart December do not meet this draft threshold. Colours saturate beyond ±300 mm; hover for exact values and quality-control status. The year 2026 is partial.

Why this chart? The heatmap makes repeated patterns and unusual months easy to scan. Colour shows the sign and size of the anomaly. Grey represents unavailable data, while near-white represents a measured value near the baseline.

Look beyond the average year

Compare annual totals in 1970–1997 and 1998–2025. The centre line is the median; each box contains the middle half of years. A taller box means a larger spread.

Loading rainfall distributions...

The typical year is drier in four of the five displayed cities. Sample medians fall in Adelaide, Hobart, Melbourne and Perth, and rise slightly in Darwin. Perth’s median falls from 733 to 672 mm (about 8%). These are available-year comparisons; unequal coverage and pending quality control limit interpretation.

How to read this chart

Dots show individual years. Whiskers reach the most extreme observations within 1.5 times the box height beyond its edges. Only complete 12-month years are included. Selectable cities have at least 15 complete years in each period; sample counts appear above the boxes. Pending quality-control months are identified in tooltips. Missing years may affect comparisons; this is not a statistical significance test.

Why this chart? Box position compares typical totals; box height compares the middle spread. A lower median does not necessarily mean greater variability. The overlapping boxes and annual points show why one summary value cannot describe every year.

snow and fire

Beyond the averages

Climate records also describe conditions associated with fire weather and alpine snow. These measures provide different perspectives: local fire-weather changes across Australia, and a long snow-depth record from one alpine site.

Changing fire-weather conditions

Compare July 1987–June 2024 with July 1950–June 1987. Larger flames show larger changes. Orange means an increase; blue means a decrease. Each flame represents a selected local grid cell.

Loading fire-weather changes...

All 22 selected locations show increases in the supplied values. These local samples do not establish a nationwide increase everywhere, or the number of fires. The CSV labels percentage change while the report describes changes in days; numerical day-count conclusions remain unresolved.

How to read this chart

Dangerous days have a Forest Fire Danger Index above the local 90th percentile over the full period. Fire symbols show 22 selected locations: three per state and the Northern Territory, and one in the ACT to avoid overlap. Each uses the nearest valid source grid cell, not a statewide average or weather-station observation. Hover for the location and change. Symbol area represents magnitude; these are fire-weather conditions, not bushfire counts. Zero change has no visible symbol.

Units follow the source CSV header: percentage change, not additional days per year. The report's map describes change in days, so this metadata discrepancy must be resolved before interpreting these values as day counts.

Why this chart? Flame area represents the magnitude, while colour distinguishes direction. Symbols are geographically located samples, not state averages. Orange-only symbols in this selection do not mean decreases are absent from the full grid.

How deep was the snow?

At Spencers Creek, each annual value is the highest recorded snow depth. Blue bands mark 100 cm intervals. The dashed line summarises the trend through 2025; the 2026 record is incomplete.

Loading snow depths...

Lower average peaks, with substantial year-to-year variation. Mean annual recorded maxima fall from 201.9 cm in 1954–1989 to 190.5 cm in 1990–2025—about 6%. This descriptive comparison is for Spencers Creek only. Irregular readings may miss actual seasonal peaks; 2026 is excluded.

How to read this chart

Blue bands mark 100 cm depth intervals. Hover for the recorded maximum, its date and the number of readings that year. Measurements are irregular, so the true seasonal peak may be missed. The incomplete 2026 record is excluded from the trend.

This is snow depth on the ground at one alpine location, not snowfall totals or an Australian average.

Why this chart? The banded area chart shows annual peak height on a common zero baseline. The dashed regression line summarises the full record through 2025; it does not imply every successive year had less snow or establish a cause.

A different snow season every year

Each ridge traces recorded snow depth through one year, from 1954 at the top to 2025 below. Measure peak height from that year’s thin zero line. All ridges use the same blue and depth scale.

Loading snow seasons...

Peak depth alone does not describe a snow season. Compare profiles with similar peaks to see how their recorded buildup and decline differ. Ridges show observation histories, but measurement gaps prevent precise claims about season length or snow-free dates.

How to read this chart

Shows 1954–2025; incomplete 2026 is omitted. Lines connect irregular observations without filling missing dates with zero. Ridge widths do not establish exact season lengths. All ridges use the same blue; height above each zero line shows depth. Spencers Creek is one alpine location, not an Australian snowfall average.

Why this chart? Aligning years to the same months makes seasonal shapes comparable. Measure height from each ridge’s own zero line, not from the bottom of the entire chart. The overlapping rows save space, although they can obscure details; hover for recorded values.

What could come next?

Historical observations show what has happened. Climate projections explore what could happen under different emissions pathways. Read them as conditional long-term outcomes, rather than forecasts for a particular year.

These are older CMIP5 projections. The model generation and source release are documented below.

Compare the emissions pathways

Projected annual mean temperatures for successive 20-year periods. Colours identify the four pathways in the legend. Hover for the station, period and number of available models.

Loading temperature projections...

The pathways describe different possible futures. Compare scenarios within the same city and period, then look at the model ranges. The displayed medians use different model counts across scenarios, so their difference also reflects different model samples—not only emissions.

How to read this chart

Values are projected 20-year averages, not forecasts for individual years. Model counts vary between scenarios (4, 6 or 8); hover for the station and model count. Ranges show model spread, not confidence intervals or all possible outcomes. These are mean temperatures, unlike the daytime maximum temperatures in the preceding chart.

Why this chart? Points and intervals show a typical model result alongside model spread. Colours identify pathways, not likelihood. The vertical ranges are not confidence intervals; these older CMIP5 outputs are conditional projections, not forecasts for individual years.

Putting the records together

One climate story, different regional experiences

The strongest national signal here is sustained warming. Local rainfall comparisons are more mixed, while the Spencers Creek record shows large annual swings alongside lower average recorded peaks in the later half of its history.

Read the measures in context

Temperature anomalies, rainfall totals, snow depth and fire-weather indices describe different things. Together they broaden the picture, but these charts alone do not prove a causal link between individual changes.

Compare fairly

Keep the baseline, season, location and observation coverage in view. Historical records describe measured conditions; projections describe conditional futures. Local patterns should not be treated as national averages.
